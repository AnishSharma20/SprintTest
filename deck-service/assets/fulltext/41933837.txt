A B S T R A C T
Background: Chronic musculoskeletal (MSK) pain is highly prevalent in older adults and the leading cause of disability. Long-chain 
omega-3 (ω-3) polyunsaturated fatty acids have anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties, yet intake and circulating levels are low in 
older US adults. Krill oil, a phospholipid-bound source of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) that also contains 
astaxanthin and choline, may represent a feasible nutrition-based intervention for chronic MSK pain.
Objectives: To evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of krill oil supplementation in older adults with chronic MSK pain and to 
characterize changes in ω-3 status and exploratory pain outcomes.
Methods: A 12-wk, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial in 40 community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 y with moderate 
chronic MSK pain. Participants were stratified by sex and randomly assigned 1:1 to krill oil (4 g/d; 1288 mg EPA+DHA) or placebo. 
Primary outcomes were feasibility metrics (recruitment, retention, adherence, and acceptability). Secondary outcomes included bio-
markers of ω-3 status and inflammation. Exploratory outcomes included pain intensity, pain interference, pain distribution, and global 
improvement.
Results: Recruitment targets were met within 8 mo; retention was 95%, adherence exceeded prespecified thresholds (>70%) in both 
groups, and overall acceptability was rated 7.7 ± 2.3 of 10. Krill oil supplementation increased the ω-3 index from 4.3% to 7.4%, 
approaching levels consistent with lower cardiometabolic risk (≥8%), whereas the placebo group showed no meaningful change. Pre-
liminary analyses showed trends favoring krill oil for reduced pain intensity (ΔKO – ΔP = ‒1.05), fewer pain sites (rate ratio: 0.59; 95% 
confidence interval: 0.41, 0.84), and global improvement (adjusted odds ratio: 4.35; 95% confidence interval: 0.97, 19.55). No serious 
adverse events occurred.
Conclusions: Krill oil supplementation was feasible and acceptable among older US adults with chronic MSK pain. These findings support 
a fully powered randomized controlled trial to evaluate krill oil as a nutritional strategy for pain management in aging populations. 
This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT06580912.
Keywords: chronic pain, ω-3 fatty acids, dietary supplements, feasibility studies
Introduction
Chronic musculoskeletal (MSK) pain is disproportionately 
prevalent among older adults [1] and is the leading cause of 
disability globally [2]. Pain management in this population re-
mains particularly challenging and often ineffective,
complicated by multimorbidity, age-related physiological 
changes, and concerns related to polypharmacy [3]. Opioid 
analgesics used to manage moderate-to-severe pain carry the 
risk of adverse effects such as dependence and tolerance, 
cognitive impairments, and drug-drug interactions in older 
adults, highlighting the need for non-pharmacological

approaches [3-5]. Nutrition-based interventions are particularly 
appealing as they are non-invasive, cost-effective, highly indi-
vidualized, and generally low risk. Despite the growing burden 
of chronic pain in aging populations [6], safe and scalable 
nutrition strategies that target MSK pain remain underexplored.
Most older adults in the United States do not meet the rec-
ommended intakes of omega (ω)-3 long-chain PUFAs (LC-
PUFAs), and <2% exhibit circulating levels associated with a 
reduced risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality [7-9]. Due 
to their well-recognized anti-inflammatory properties [10], ω-3 
LC-PUFAs, particularly EPA and DHA, have been increasingly 
studied for their potential analgesic effects. Mechanistically, ω-3 
LC-PUFAs may modulate pain both peripherally and centrally by 
promoting specialized pro-resolving mediators, dampening 
neuronal excitability, and supporting nerve repair and neuro-
immune homeostasis [11]. Additionally, ω-3 LC-PUFAs may 
support MSK pain specifically by attenuating catabolic processes 
and the progression of osteoarthritis (OA) [12,13]. 
Meta-analyses of ω-3 supplementation trials have found mod-
erate, clinically meaningful reductions for inflammatory pain 
[11,14,15], whereas evidence for OA-related pain remains 
limited and inconclusive [11]. As suggested by Xie et al. [11], 
the lack of statistically significant effects observed in some OA 
trials may reflect the complex and multifactorial nature of 
OA-related pain, including low-grade inflammation, mechanical 
drivers of pain, and the use of multidimensional outcome mea-
sures [11]. Moreover, relatively few trials have specifically 
examined OA populations.
Krill oil (Ephausia superba) is a marine source of ω-3 LC-
PUFAs in which EPA and DHA are predominantly bound to 
phospholipids rather than triglycerides or ethyl esters. This 
structural difference is thought to enhance bioavailability and 
tissue uptake [16]. On the contrary, the absolute EPA and DHA 
content per gram in krill oil is often lower than that of concen-
trated fish oils [17,18], which may offset its benefits by the need 
for higher volumes to achieve the desired intake of EPA and 
DHA, potentially compromising tolerability and acceptability, 
especially in older adults. Beyond ω-3 fatty acids, krill oil con-
tains additional bioactive compounds that may be beneficial for 
pain. Krill is naturally rich in astaxanthin, a xanthophyll carot-
enoid with potent anti-inflammatory/antioxidant, ger-
oprotective, and analgesic properties [19,20]. In addition, krill 
oil provides choline as phosphatidylcholine, an essential 
nutrient involved in neurotransmission, cell signaling, lipid 
metabolism, and homocysteine regulation. As a precursor to 
acetylcholine, choline may influence cholinergic pathways 
involved in pain modulation [21] and plays an important role in 
skeletal muscle metabolism and musculoskeletal health [22]. 
Yet, choline is poorly consumed by older adults in the United 
States [23,24]. Although krill oil supplementation has demon-
strated favorable effects on knee OA [25], older adults have 
been underrepresented in these trials. As such, the feasibility, 
acceptability, and potential effects of krill oil on chronic MSK 
pain in older adults remain unknown.
The Krill Oil for Pain in Elders (KOPE) pilot study was a 
randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial designed to 
evaluate the feasibility of conducting a larger efficacy trial in 
older adults with chronic MSK pain. Consistent with the 
CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials, the primary 
objectives of the KOPE pilot were to assess feasibility metrics,
including recruitment, retention, adherence, acceptability, 
and data completeness, to inform the design of a future fully 
powered randomized controlled trial. Our secondary objec-
tives were to characterize changes in ω-3 status and inflam-
mation, and we performed preliminary analyses of pain 
outcomes.
Methods
Trial design
This pilot trial used a randomized, double-blind, placebo-
controlled, parallel-group design conducted over a 12-wk 
intervention period. Participants completed 3 in-person study 
visits: a baseline visit prior to randomization (week 0), a mid-
intervention follow-up visit (week 6), and a final assessment at 
the end of the intervention period (week 12). The trial was 
designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 
extension for randomized pilot and feasibility trials (see Sup-
plementary Checklist) [26]. All research was conducted at the 
University of Florida. The study was approved by the University 
of Florida Institutional Review Board (#202400581) and con-
ducted under an active US Food and Drug Administration 
Investigational New Drug application. All participants provided 
written informed consent prior to participation. The 
trial is registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06580912); full 
details are available at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/ 
NCT06580912.
Participants
A total of 40 community-dwelling older adults were 
recruited from Gainesville, FL, and neighboring areas primarily 
through postcard mailings and social media advertisements. 
Participants were first screened for preliminary eligibility by 
telephone and subsequently invited for an in-person screening 
visit, during which written informed consent and eligibility 
assessments were completed. Eligible participants completed 
the baseline assessment within 30 d of the screening visit prior 
to randomization. If >30 d elapsed between screening and 
baseline, eligibility assessments were repeated to confirm 
continued eligibility.
Eligible participants were males and females aged ≥60 y who 
reported moderate pain (≥4 on a 0–10 scale) [27] in the hips, 
knees, or lower back on most days for the past 3 mo and 
demonstrated moderate mobility limitations. Exclusion criteria 
were designed to minimize risks and reduce confounding. These 
included: bleeding or coagulation disorders; use of anticoagu-
lants, full-dose aspirin, opioids, high-dose nonsteroidal 
anti-inflammatory drugs, selective serotonin reuptake in-
hibitors, and complementary medicines/supplements that may 
pose a risk of food-drug interaction (e.g., St. John’s Wort) or 
may affect study results; ω-3 supplementation (<3 mo) or >2 
servings per week of fatty fish intake; seafood allergy; clinically 
significant or uncontrolled medical conditions; substance use 
history; severe mobility impairment; or planned surgery during 
the study period.
Randomization and blinding
Participants were stratified by sex and randomly assigned in 
a 1:1 ratio to receive either krill oil or a placebo. Randomization

was performed by (YC-A) using a computer-generated allocation 
sequence with block randomization using varying block sizes (2, 
4, and 6) to ensure balance between groups. Allocation 
concealment was maintained by using identical-appearing study 
capsules and bottles. Except for (YC-A), participants, in-
vestigators, and study staff who interacted with participants 
were blinded to group assignment throughout the trial. (YC-A) 
was not involved in patient recruitment, enrollment, or clinical 
assessments.
Intervention
Participants assigned to the treatment group received 4 g/ 
d of krill oil (Superba Boost, Aker BioMarine Antarctic US LLC) 
in the form of 4 1000 mg capsules, providing a total of 1288 mg/ 
d EPA+DHA, 0.45 mg astaxanthin, and 320 mg choline daily. 
Placebo capsules matched the krill oil capsules in appearance, 
odor, and taste, containing 1000 mg of mixed vegetable oil, 
comprised olive oil, corn oil, palm oil, and medium-chain tri-
glycerides in a ratio of 4:4:3:2 (~31% SFAs, 46% MUFAs, and 
22% PUFAs, with no EPA or DHA). This mixture resembles the 
fatty acid composition of a typical Western diet [28,29]. Within 
the context of the whole diet, the small amount of mixed fats 
consumed in the placebo is not expected to have any indepen-
dent effects. Participants were instructed to take the assigned 
capsules once daily, with a meal, for 12 wk and to maintain their 
usual diet and physical activity patterns throughout the study 
period. Tolerance was assessed weekly for the first 3 wk, and 
dosage and timing were adjusted as needed. If participants re-
ported digestive discomfort or difficulty tolerating 4 capsules 
per day, they were advised to temporarily reduce intake to 1 
capsule and gradually increase by 1 capsule daily as tolerated 
until the full daily dose was reached. If taking all capsules at 
once was not well tolerated, the dose could also be divided 
across the day (e.g., 2 capsules in the morning and 2 in the 
evening).
Data collection
Study data were collected and managed using REDCap 
(Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted at the University of 
Florida [30,31].
• Feasibility outcomes (primary): feasibility was determined
by meeting pre-selected criteria, which included achieving
≥80% of the recruitment goal within 12 mo, maintaining 
<20% attrition, and achieving >70% adherence to the 
intervention. The Medication Acceptability Questionnaire 
(MAQ) was used to assess perceptions of convenience, taste 
and texture, appearance and smell, side effects, and overall 
acceptance[32].
• Biomarker outcomes (secondary): fasted whole-blood sam-
ples were obtained at each study visit. Changes in blood fatty 
acid profile were assessed from dried blood spots using gas 
chromatography (OmegaQuant Analytics) [33]. The ω-3 
index is calculated as the percentage of EPA and DHA in 
erythrocytes, and is considered a clinically relevant 
biomarker of ω-3 status that is highly responsive to dietary 
intake [34,35]. The circulating ω-6:ω-3 ratio was included for 
contextualization with prior studies, although it is no longer 
considered a preferred indicator of ω-3 status.
High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured 
at every visit as an inflammatory biomarker and for safety 
monitoring. Hs-CRP concentrations were quantified in serum 
using a high-sensitivity immunoturbidimetric assay and re-
ported in milligrams per liter.
• Clinical outcomes (exploratory): due to small sample sizes, 
pilot studies cannot adequately provide evidence of safety, 
tolerability, or effect sizes. Here, we present preliminary 
analyses focused on the primary endpoints planned for a 
future, fully powered trial: pain intensity and interference, 
and physical function. 
Average pain intensity (past week) was assessed using a 
single item from the Graded Chronic Pain Scale–Revised (0–10 
scale), which includes the 3-item Pain, Enjoyment of Life, and 
General Activity (PEG) scale used as a summary measure of 
pain intensity and interference [36]. The Western Ontario and 
McMaster Universities OA Index (WOMAC) was used to mea-
sure joint pain, stiffness, and physical function; all scores were 
normalized to a 0–100 scale [37]. The overall perceived treat-
ment response was assessed using the Patient Global Impres-
sion of Change (PGIC) [38], dichotomized as “improved” 
compared with “not improved.” Pain distribution was quanti-
fied as the total number of painful body sites using the Michigan 
Body Map (range: 0–32) [39]. Neuropathic pain symptoms 
were assessed with the painDETECT [40]. Self-reported phys-
ical function was assessed with the Pepper Assessment Tool for 
Disability, which evaluates mobility, transferring, upper ex-
tremity function, activities of daily living, and instrumental 
activities of daily living [41,42]. Performance-based physical 
function was assessed using the short physical performance 
battery (SPPB), which includes tests of balance, gait speed, and 
chair stands, with a total score ranging from 0 to 12 [43,44]. 
Handgrip strength was measured using a handheld dyna-
mometer as the highest value derived from the mean of 2 trials 
performed on each hand [45]. Functional capacity and endur-
ance were assessed using the 6-min walk test [46].
• Additional measures: sociodemographic characteristics (age, 
sex, race and ethnicity, as well as education) were self-
reported. Height and weight were measured using stan-
dardized procedures and used to calculate the BMI (in kg/ 
m 2 ). Dietary intake was assessed using the Diet History 
Questionnaire III to measure total energy intake, overall diet 
quality using the Healthy Eating Index (HEI), and dietary fat 
quality via the HEI fatty acids component. HEI scores range 
from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater adher-
ence to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans.
Sample size
This pilot study was designed to guide the design and 
implementation of large-scale trials. We planned to enroll 40 
older adults within a 12-mo period. Randomization was strati-
fied by sex to ensure balanced representation across treatment 
arms (10 males and 10 females per arm). Allowing an antici-
pated attrition rate of ~20%, we expected ~32 participants 
(~16 per group) to complete the intervention. This sample size 
is appropriate for assessing feasibility outcomes and is consis-
tent with methodological recommendations for pilot random-
ized trials, particularly when anticipated effects are small to 
moderate [47].

Statistical analysis
Analyses of feasibility outcomes were descriptive in nature 
and summarized using proportions and means with SDs. 
Changes in biomarkers and clinical outcomes were evaluated 
using mixed-effects models with fixed effects for treatment 
group, time (modeled as a categorical variable), and the group-
by-time interaction, a random intercept for each participant, 
and an independent covariance structure. All models were 
adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. For continuous outcomes, 
treatment effects are reported as adjusted between-group dif-
ferences in change from baseline (ΔKO − ΔP) with 95% confi-
dence intervals (CIs). Hs-CRP was log-transformed prior to 
analysis due to right-skewed distributions. Total pain sites, a 
count outcome, were analyzed using negative binomial mixed-
effects models and are reported as rate ratios with 95% CIs. 
The PGIC was analyzed as a binary outcome using logistic 
regression, with results reported as odds ratios and 95% CIs. 
Standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were also reported for 
continuous outcomes to aid interpretation of effect 
magnitude, calculated using the residual SD from the mixed-
effects models. Effect sizes were interpreted using conven-
tional thresholds, with values of ~0.2, ~0.5, and ~0.8 repre-
senting small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Model 
assumptions were evaluated through standard diagnostic pro-
cedures, including inspection of residual distributions and 
assessment of homoscedasticity. All analyses followed the 
intention-to-treat principle. Statistical analyses were conducted 
using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).
Results
A participant flowchart is presented in Figure 1. Recruitment 
began in January 2025 and continued through September 2025. 
During this period, 334 individuals were phone-screened for 
initial eligibility. Of these, 63 participants (18.9%) met pre-
liminary criteria and were invited to complete an in-person
screening visit. A total of 23 individuals were excluded during 
screening. The most common reasons for exclusion were SPPB 
scores outside the eligible range (>10, n = 11; <4, n = 1) and a 
history of clinically significant conditions (n = 6). One indi-
vidual declined participation during the informed consent 
process.
A total of 40 participants were subsequently randomly 
assigned to receive either krill oil (n = 20) or placebo (n = 20) in 
a sex-stratified manner (10 males and 10 females per group). All 
randomly assigned participants received their allocated inter-
vention and were included in the intention-to-treat analyses.
Participant characteristics
Baseline characteristics were generally similar between 
groups (Table 1). Participants had a mean age of 70.1 y (SD = 
5.7), were predominantly non-Hispanic White (72.5%), and had 
a mean BMI of 30.8 (SD = 6.7). The proportion of non-Hispanic 
White participants was slightly higher in the placebo group. Diet 
quality was similar between groups, although mean total energy 
intake was higher in the placebo group.
Participants reported a high pain burden, with a mean of 7.8 
(SD = 4.2) pain sites per participant (range: 2–21). The most 
common pain sites were the lower back (75.0%), hips (65.0%), 
and knees (62.5%). The lower back was also the most frequently 
reported worst pain site (30.0%) and second-worst pain site 
(12.5%), experienced for durations of 10 (IQR: 3, 16) and 5 (3, 
25) y, respectively. Participants also reported moderate to high 
pain intensity and interference, with a mean PEG score of 5.5 
(SD = 2.3) and a WOMAC score of 40.1 (SD = 13.5). Nearly half 
(47.5%) of participants were classified as having possible or 
likely neuropathic pain, with a mean PainDETECT score of 12.7 
(SD = 6.8).
Performance-based physical function tests showed moderate 
impairments, with a mean SPPB score of 8.5 (SD = 1.4), 6-min 
walk test distance of 368.0 m (SD = 85.1), and handgrip 
strength of 28.9 kg (SD = 9.7).

Feasibility outcomes
Recruitment took place over ~8 mo, with an average of 5 
participants enrolled per month. Notably, enrollment 
differed by sex. The target enrollment for females (n = 20) was 
reached in ~5.5 mo, an enrollment rate of 3.6 females per 
month. In contrast, males were enrolled at a rate of ~2.5 per 
month.
Retention throughout the study period was high, with 38 of 
40 participants (95%) completing the intervention. All partici-
pants who completed the intervention also completed all 
scheduled assessments, resulting in complete outcome data 
among completers. During the 12-wk intervention period, 2 
participants (5.0%) in the krill oil group withdrew from the 
study, resulting in 38 participants who completed the final 
assessment. One participant withdrew within 5 d of intervention 
initiation due to a migraine flare-up, and 1 participant withdrew 
for personal reasons. No participants were discontinued during 
the intervention, and no withdrawals were attributed to serious 
adverse events.
Adherence to the study supplement was high across both 
groups. Based on self-reported capsule intake (n = 37), mean 
adherence was 95.1% (SD = 5.8) in the krill oil group and 91.7% 
(SD = 12.0) among controls. Using capsule counts (n = 36), 
mean adherence was ~80.8% (SD = 9.9) in the krill oil group 
and 77.7% (SD = 12.1) among controls. When applying the 
prespecified adherence criterion of ≥70% daily capsule con-
sumption, 100% of participants in the krill oil group (n = 18) 
and 89.5% of controls (n = 17) met this threshold based on self-
report. Similarly, 83.3% of participants in the krill oil group (n = 
15) and 83.3% of controls (n = 15) met the ≥70% adherence 
threshold based on capsule counts.
The intervention’s acceptability, assessed with the Medica-
tion Acceptability Questionnaire, was generally high at the end 
of the study. Mean scores for convenience, taste, appearance, 
and tolerability ranged from ~71% to ~85%, indicating good 
overall acceptability of the study products. Perceived efficacy 
scores were lower and more variable (mean = 46.5%, SD = 
24.6). Participants also reported being generally satisfied with 
the side effects, with a mean of 8.1 of 10 (SD = 2.6), and rated 
the overall acceptability at 7.7 of 10 (SD = 2.3). No substantial 
differences were observed between study groups (all P > 0.17).
Adverse events
In total, 30 participants (75%) reported ≥1 adverse event 
during the study, with 16 (80%) in the control group and 14 
(70%) in the krill oil group. A total of 92 adverse events were 
recorded (controls: 58; the krill oil group: 34). No serious 
adverse events occurred in either group. Gastrointestinal 
symptoms were the most frequently reported adverse events, 
including diarrhea, constipation, flatulence, abdominal pain, 
and dyspepsia. Changes in appetite were also reported: 
decreased appetite by 6 participants (placebo: n = 5; krill oil: n
= 1) and increased appetite by 3 participants (all in the control 
group). Unanticipated adverse events were more frequent in the 
control group (11 events) than in the krill oil group (3 events). 
Adverse events considered related to the intervention were also 
more frequent among controls (placebo: 5; krill oil: 2), all of 
which were gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhea, 
dyspepsia, flatulence, and abdominal pain. One participant in 
the krill oil group discontinued the intervention due to a 
migraine flare-up; no participants in the control group withdrew 
due to adverse events.

Biomarkers
The baseline ω-3 index (adjusted mean: 4.3%; 95% CI: 3.8%, 
4.8%) was low among participants, consistent with limited di-
etary intake of ω-3 fatty acids. Changes in the ω-3 index are 
shown in Table 2 and Figure 2. The adjusted between-group 
mean difference in change of 3.0% (95% CI: 1.9, 4.0), corre-
sponding to a very large effect (Cohen’s d = 4.40). As expected, 
the ω-3 index increased significantly only in the krill oil group, 
with a marked increase evident by 6 wk (7.3%; 95% CI: 6.7%, 
7.8%) and remaining stable through the final assessment (7.4%; 
95% CI: 6.9%, 8.0%). In contrast, the placebo group showed no 
meaningful change over time (range: 4.5–4.7%). A similar 
pattern was seen with the ω-6:ω-3 ratio. No substantial changes 
in hs-CRP were observed.
Clinical pain outcomes
Across clinical pain outcomes, changes over time favored the 
krill oil group (Table 3). On the Graded Chronic Pain Sca-
le–Revised average pain intensity item, the between-group dif-
ference in change was − 1.05 points (95% CI: − 2.72, 0.62; 
Cohen’s d = − 0.77), corresponding to greater within-group pain 
intensity reductions in the krill oil group compared with placebo 
(− 1.20 compared with − 0.15, respectively); shown in
Figure 3A. For pain interference, assessed using the PEG scale, 
the between-group difference in change was small (− 0.09; 95% 
CI: − 2.00, 1.83; Cohen’s d = − 0.06), consistent with similar 
within-group changes in the krill oil and placebo groups (− 0.76 
compared with − 0.68, respectively).
Similar patterns were observed for WOMAC outcomes. The 
between-group difference in change was − 5.0 for pain (95% CI:
− 18.9, 9.0; Cohen’s d = − 0.46), − 8.7 for stiffness (95% CI:
− 25.6, 8.1; Cohen’s d = − 0.66), and − 0.9 for physical function 
(95% CI: − 13.1, 11.2; Cohen’s d = − 0.13). For the WOMAC 
total score, the between-group difference in change was − 2.4 
(95% CI: − 14.0, 9.2; Cohen’s d = − 0.32). Within-group changes 
are presented in Table 3.
Beyond pain intensity and interference, participants 
receiving krill oil demonstrated a greater reduction in pain 
distribution, with a mean decrease of 3.5 total pain sites 
compared with 0.4 sites in the placebo group (Figure 3B). This 
corresponded to a 41% lower rate of pain sites at follow-up (rate 
ratio: 0.59; 95% CI: 0.41, 0.84).
Finally, consistent with these patterns, a greater proportion 
of participants in the krill oil group reported overall improve-
ment on the PGIC compared with placebo (66.7% compared 
with 40.0%; adjusted odds ratio: 4.35; 95% CI: 0.97, 19.55).

Discussion
This pilot randomized controlled trial shows that a 12-wk 
protocol of 4 g/d krill oil supplementation is feasible and 
acceptable among older adults with moderate-to-severe chronic 
MSK pain. Although previous studies have shown promise for 
the analgesic effects of krill oil, most have focused on middle-
aged adults, condition- and site-specific pain (i.e., knee OA), 
with mild-to-moderate symptom severity, and have been con-
ducted outside the United States [25,48–52]. In contrast, we 
targeted older adults with chronic MSK pain and pain-related 
interference on physical function, a population with a greater 
burden of pain, comorbidities, and risk factors, and for whom 
non-pharmacological pain management strategies are particu-
larly needed. Adherence exceeding the predetermined feasi-
bility threshold, together with favorable acceptability, suggests 
that a 4 g/d dosing strategy is acceptable and implementable in 
this population.
The feasibility outcomes from this pilot inform and support 
the design of future fully powered randomized trials. Recruit-
ment targets were met ahead of schedule, retention was high, 
adherence exceeded the prespecified thresholds, and overall 
acceptability of the intervention was favorable. We found that 
females were enrolled more rapidly than males, which led us to 
modify recruitment materials specifically for males. This sug-
gests that diverse, targeted recruitment strategies may be 
needed to ensure balanced sex representation in a larger trial. 
Retention and adherence exceeded the prespecified criteria, 
supporting the feasibility of a longer intervention period and 
suggesting that a higher adherence threshold of ≥80% may be a 
reasonable benchmark.
The robust increase in the ω-3 index in the krill oil group 
confirms biological uptake and supports its use as an objective 
adherence biomarker. Mean ω-3 index values increased from 
~4% at baseline to ~8% by follow-up, representing a clinically 
meaningful change from a higher-risk to a lower-risk range [34, 
53]. This response is consistent with dose-response predictions 
by Walker et al. [54], which estimate that with a baseline ω-3 
index ~4%–5%, ~1.2–1.4 g/d of EPA+DHA (as triglycerides) 
would be required to reach an ω-3 index of 8%. Although 
Walker’s model does not account for phospholipid-bound ω-3 
sources such as krill oil, the model-based prediction closely

the ω-3 index increased over 6 wk and stabilized thereafter, 
despite erythrocytes having a ~120-d lifespan. It may be that 
incorporation into erythrocyte membranes may reach a steady 
state earlier than complete red blood cell turnover. Whether 
longer supplementation durations would lead to further in-
creases in the ω-3 index warrants investigation with extended 
follow-up. On the contrary, habitual intake of fatty fish with this 
supplementation protocol may achieve the desired ω-3 index 
threshold of ≥8% [55].
Although the study was not designed or powered to test ef-
ficacy, preliminary analyses of clinical pain outcomes consis-
tently favored krill oil supplementation across multiple pain 
measures and domains. Participants receiving krill oil demon-
strated larger reductions in average pain intensity, musculo-
skeletal pain and stiffness, pain distribution, and a greater 
likelihood of reporting overall improvement compared with 
placebo. These trends can be expected to continue with a longer 
supplementation period, as a meta-analysis of ω-3 supplemen-
tation randomized controlled trials indicated that benefits are 
noticeable within 1 mo and continue to improve by 6 mo [11]. 
These patterns suggest the potential efficacy of krill oil for more 
generalized, multisite, and high-impact pain phenotypes 
commonly seen in older adults. Reductions in multisite pain may 
be particularly relevant for older adults, as widespread pain is 
strongly associated with mobility limitations and disability. 
Importantly, effect size estimates are presented to aid interpre-
tation and considerations for future trials, and should not be 
interpreted as evidence of efficacy.
On the contrary, krill oil supplementation did not result in 
meaningful improvements in self-reported or performance-
based physical function over the 12-wk intervention period. As 
with pain interference, improvements in musculoskeletal health 
and physical function likely require longer intervention periods 
and sustained biological adaptation. ω-3 supplementation trials 
have shown limited and inconsistent evidence of efficacy for 
muscle mass, strength, and functional outcomes in older adults, 
particularly in interventions under 6 mo duration [56–59].
Although studies with krill oil are limited, a randomized 
controlled trial in generally healthy older adults showed that 4 
g/d of krill oil for 6 mo significantly increased knee extensor 
strength, handgrip strength, and vastus lateralis muscle thick-
ness, without improvements in SPPB performance [60,61]. In 
addition to intervention duration, improvements in physical 
function may be more difficult to achieve in older adults with 
high-impact chronic MSK pain.
Several limitations should be considered. As a pilot feasibility 
study with a relatively small sample size and short duration, it 
cannot inform the efficacy or safety of the intervention. The 
participants were recruited from a single academic center and 
surrounding areas and were mostly non-Hispanic White, which 
may limit generalizability to broader, more diverse populations. 
On the contrary, the participants in this study exhibit diet pat-
terns and baseline ω-3 index levels that align with those in the 
general older population. Baseline dietary data confirmed low 
consumption of oily fish, with no participant reporting >1 
serving per week (data not shown). Although adherence was 
high, as confirmed by self-report, capsule counts, and ω-3 index 
levels, long-term adherence may differ.
In conclusion, this pilot trial demonstrates that krill oil 
supplementation at 4 g/d is feasible and acceptable among 
older adults with chronic MSK pain. The feasibility and 
adherence data, together with consistent preliminary signals 
across pain outcomes, support and inform the design of a 
larger, fully powered randomized controlled trial. Given the 
burden of chronic pain in aging populations and the limitations 
of current pharmacologic pain management in older adults, 
krill oil may represent a promising, generally low risk, first-line 
or adjunctive non-pharmacological strategy to support pain 
management in aging. The findings support the practicality of 
implementing a krill oil nutritional intervention in this high-
risk population.
